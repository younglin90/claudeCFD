# Generalized Riemann Problem Method for the Kapila Model of Compressible Multiphase Flows

Tuowei Chen 1 and Zhifang Du\* 2

(\*Electronic mail: du zhifang@iapcm.ac.cn)

A second-order accurate and robust numerical scheme is developed for the Kapila model to simulate compressible multiphase flows. The scheme is formulated within the finite volume framework with the generalized Riemann problem (GRP) solver employed as the cornerstone. Besides Riemann solutions, the GRP solver provides time derivatives of flow variables at cell interfaces, achieving second-order accuracy in time within a single stage. The use of the GRP solver enhances the capability of the resulting scheme to handle the stiffness of the Kapila model in two ways. First, the coupled values, i.e., Riemann solutions and time derivatives, give the cell interface values of flow variables at the new time level, yielding an approximation to the cell average of the velocity divergence at the new time level in a computational step. This allows a semi-implicit time discretization to the stiff source term of the volume fraction equation. Second, the effects of source terms are directly included in the numerical flux via the computation of time derivatives. The resulting numerical flux is able to capture the physics of interactions between phases, and the robustness of the scheme is therefore further improved. Several challenging numerical experiments are conducted to demonstrate the good performance of the proposed finite volume scheme. In particular, a test case with a nonlinear smooth solution is designed to verify the numerical accuracy.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Compressible multiphase flows appear in numerous scientific and engineering disciplines, including astrophysics1,18,29, cavitation flows24,35,43, and deflagration-to-detonation transitions9,36,40. There has been an enormous amount of successful applications of sharp interface methods in numerical simulations involving compressible multiphase flows8,13,15,46 . In contrast, this work concentrates on the development of a shock-capturing scheme that is capable of capturing interfaces between pure fluids as well as fluid mixtures. To achieve this, the Kapila model is employed in the present work for its capability of effectively modeling the underlying physical process of compressible multiphase flows19,20,42 .

In Ref. 4, Baer and Nunziato proposed a seven-equation model, known as the Baer-Nunziato (BN) model, to describe the dynamics of multiphase flows, specifically in solid granular explosives mixed with gaseous products. The BN model includes mass, momentum, and energy balance laws for each individual phase. The six governing equations are augmented by a seventh one modeling the gaseous volume fraction evolution. After being further extended to modeling general multiphase flows<sup>38</sup>, much attention has been devoted to developing numerical schemes for the seven-equation model2,3,21,26,45. A five-equation model, known as the Kapila model, was proposed in Ref. 19 as a reduced version of the well-known BN model. This reduction is based on the assumption that, in many practical applications, the interphase exchanges of momentum and kinetic energy occur rapidly. The Kapila model emerges as the asymptotic limit in cases of strong interphase drag and negligible compaction viscosity<sup>19</sup>. The Kapila model consists of continuity equations of each phase, conservation laws of the total momentum and the total energy, and the volume fraction equation with a stiff source term that represents the interaction between phases. The sound speed of the mixture is defined by the Wood formula<sup>49</sup>. The same five-equation model was also derived in Ref. 31. Although this model was derived from the seven-equation model for mixtures, it can be applied to numerical simulations of interfaces separating either pure fluids or fluid mixtures<sup>42</sup> .

After its introduction, the Kapila model has been widely used in numerical simulations of compressible multiphase flows22,33,47. Nevertheless, such efforts face two distinct difficulties. In the first place, the stiff source term in the volume fraction equation, which arises as the result of the stiff limit of the relaxation process in the original seven-equation model, is

particularly difficult to approximate and makes the bound-preserving property of the volume fraction a challenging task. Furthermore, the non-monotonic behavior of the Wood sound speed in relation to volume fractions leads to Mach number oscillations in the numerical diffusion zone of an interface. To mitigate these difficulties, Saurel et al. proposed a projection-relaxation scheme34,39, which relies on resolving subcell structures of the numerical solution. Later, this method was further enhanced by relaxing the pressure equilibrium assumption<sup>44</sup> .

The present paper provides an alternative approach to address the stiffness of the Kapila model. To accomplish this, we highlight the significance of the generalized Riemann problem (GRP) solver in developing high-resolution and robust numerical schemes for compressible fluid flows. The GRP solver is a typical representative of Lax-Wendroff type flux solvers, which allows the corresponding numerical scheme to achieve second-order accuracy in time within a single-stage, comparing with the Runge-Kutta type schemes. The GRP solver was initially proposed in the context of gas dynamics5,6, and a more straightforward and efficient version of the GRP solver was later developed in the Eulerian framework<sup>7</sup> . Under the acoustic assumption, the GRP can be linearized and thus solved approximately12,30,32,48 . Developing a GRP solver for the Kapila model to address its stiffness is the focus of this paper.

In comparison with the Riemann problem, the GRP is formulated as an initial value problem (IVP) of hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs) with piecewise smooth initial data in place of piecewise constant ones used in the Riemann problem. The GRP features waves emanating from the initial discontinuity that no longer travel along straight lines, and provides instantaneous time derivatives of flow variables along with Riemann solutions. The fundamental approach adopted to solve the GRP involves the so-called Lax-Wendroff procedure<sup>23</sup>, which employs governing equations to quantify the relation between the time derivative of the solution and its initial spatial variation<sup>27</sup>. The coupled values, i.e., Riemann solutions and instantaneous time derivatives, provide evolutionary cell interface values of flow variables. This feature has been successfully used to develop compact data reconstructions11,16,17, which rely on approximating gradients of flow variables at the new time level in a computational step. Additionally, it is illustrated that time derivatives incorporate the transversal effect of the flow field into numerical fluxes, which facilitates an accurate resolution to multi-dimensional effects in numerical computations<sup>25</sup> .

Regarding the Kapila model, the application of the GRP solver enhances the robustness of the numerical scheme in two ways. The first notable advantage is that the source term in the volume fraction equation can be evolved using a semi-implicit discretization. This is possible since the GRP solver provides cell interface values of the flow variables at the new time level, which, together with the Gauss-Green formula, give the cell average of velocity divergence at the new time level. When specifically implementing the Crank-Nicolson evolution, a time step restriction to preserve the bound of volume fractions can be established. Second, thanks to the Lax-Wendroff procedure, which makes thorough use of governing PDEs, the compaction or expansion effect carried by the source term in the volume fraction equation is fully resolved by utilizing time derivatives at cell interfaces. As a result, the capability of numerical fluxes to capture interactions between phases is considerably improved.

This paper is organized as follows. The GRP-based finite volume scheme for the Kapila model is constructed in Section II. In addition, the time step restriction to preserve the bound of volume fractions is established therein. The corresponding GRP solver is developed in Section III. Finally, we present results of several numerical experiments in Section IV to demonstrate the good performance of the proposed scheme. Several concluding remarks are put in Section V.

## II. FINITE VOLUME SCHEME FOR THE KAPILA MODEL

This section is dedicated to the development of a finite volume scheme for the Kapila model. In two-phase cases, the governing equations of the Kapila model are

$$\frac{\partial \zeta_1 \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\zeta_1 \rho \mathbf{u}) = 0, \tag{1a}$$

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u}) = 0, \tag{1b}$$

$$\frac{\partial \rho \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u} \otimes \mathbf{u}) + \nabla p = 0, \tag{1c}$$

$$\frac{\partial \rho E}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot [(\rho E + p)\mathbf{u}] = 0, \tag{1d}$$

$$\frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \alpha_1 = -\alpha_1 \alpha_2 \frac{\rho_1 c_1^2 - \rho_2 c_2^2}{\alpha_1 \rho_2 c_2^2 + \alpha_2 \rho_1 c_1^2} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}, \tag{1e}$$

where ρ is the mixture density, E is the total energy per unit mass, p is the pressure, and u is the velocity. In two space dimensions, u = (u, v) <sup>⊤</sup>. For each phase k (k = 1, 2), α<sup>k</sup> and ζ<sup>k</sup> represent the volume and mass fractions, satisfying

$$\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 1, \quad \zeta_1 + \zeta_2 = 1.$$
 (2)

Note that given the continuity equation (1a) for the phase k = 1, the other one for the phase k = 2 is replaced by the conservation law of the mixture mass (1b). The phase density ρ<sup>k</sup> is defined by

$$\rho_k = \frac{\zeta_k}{\alpha_k} \rho,$$

which leads to ρ = α1ρ<sup>1</sup> + α2ρ2. The total energy of the mixture is

$$E = \frac{1}{2} \left| \mathbf{u} \right|^2 + e,\tag{3}$$

where e is the specific internal energy of the mixture. The thermodynamical variables of each phase follow its Gibbs relation

$$de_k = T_k ds_k - p d\tau_k, (4)$$

where e<sup>k</sup> and T<sup>k</sup> are the specific internal energy and the temperature of each phase k and τ<sup>k</sup> = 1 ρk is the specific volume. From Gibbs relation (4), the specific internal energy of each phase depends on other thermodynamical variables via the equation of state (EOS)

$$e_k = e_k(p, \rho_k). (5)$$

In this paper, we consider the stiffened gas EOS:

$$p = (\gamma_k - 1)\rho_k e_k - \gamma_k \pi_k, \tag{6}$$

where γ<sup>k</sup> > 1 is the specific heat ratio and π<sup>k</sup> ≥ 0 is a material parameter. The sound speed of each phase k is defined as

$${c_k}^2 = \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho_k}\right)_{s_k},\,$$

where s<sup>k</sup> is the entropy. The mixture sound speed is defined by the Wood formula<sup>49</sup> as

$$\frac{1}{\rho c^2} = \frac{\alpha_1}{\rho_1 c_1^2} + \frac{\alpha_2}{\rho_2 c_2^2}. (7)$$

For stiffened gases, the sound speed of each phase k is explicitly defined by

$$c_k^2 = \frac{\gamma_k(p + \pi_k)}{\rho_k}. (8)$$

In the remainder of this section, the GRP-based finite volume scheme for the Kapila model together with the corresponding semi-implicit time discretization of the volume fraction equation is developed. Afterwards, this section is closed by analyzing the bound-preserving property of volume fractions. To make the present paper self-containing, the linear data reconstruction procedure employed in Refs. 6 and 7 is put into Appendix A.

#### A. Finite volume discretization of governing equations

In order to apply the finite volume scheme for hyperbolic balance laws, add  $\alpha_1 \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}$  on both sides of (1e) and rewrite it as

$$\frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\alpha_1 \mathbf{u}) = \frac{\alpha_1 \rho_2 c_2^2}{\alpha_1 \rho_2 c_2^2 + (1 - \alpha_1) \rho_1 c_1^2} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}. \tag{9}$$

With (9), rewrite the governing equations (1a)-(1e) in two space dimensions as

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{U}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial y} = \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{U}, \nabla \mathbf{U}), \tag{10}$$

where

$$\mathbf{U} = [\zeta_{1}\rho, \rho, \rho u, \rho v, \rho E, \alpha_{1}]^{\top},$$

$$\mathbf{F} = [\zeta_{1}\rho u, \rho u, \rho u^{2} + p, \rho u v, (\rho E + p)u, u\alpha_{1}]^{\top},$$

$$\mathbf{G} = [\zeta_{1}\rho v, \rho v, \rho u v, \rho v^{2} + p, (\rho E + p)v, v\alpha_{1}]^{\top},$$

$$\mathbf{K} = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, \alpha_{1}K\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial u}\right)]^{\top},$$

$$(11)$$

and

$$K = \frac{\rho_2 c_2^2}{\alpha_1 \rho_2 c_2^2 + (1 - \alpha_1) \rho_1 c_1^2}.$$
 (12)

Divide the computational domain  $\Omega$  into uniform rectangular cells  $\{\Omega_{ij}\}$  as

$$\Omega = \bigcup_{i,j} \Omega_{ij},$$

where

$$\Omega_{ij} = [x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}, x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}] \times [y_{j-\frac{1}{2}}, y_{j+\frac{1}{2}}].$$

The cell size is denoted as  $\Delta x = x_{i+\frac{1}{2}} - x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}$  and  $\Delta y = y_{j+\frac{1}{2}} - y_{j-\frac{1}{2}}$ . Denote  $x_i = \frac{1}{2}(x_{i-\frac{1}{2}} + x_{i+\frac{1}{2}})$  and  $y_j = \frac{1}{2}(y_{j-\frac{1}{2}} + y_{j+\frac{1}{2}})$ . By integrating governing equations (10) in the space-time

control volume  $\Omega_{ij} \times [t^n, t^{n+1}]$ , the GRP-based finite volume scheme for (10) is

$$\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{ij}^{n+1} = \overline{\mathbf{U}}_{ij}^{n} - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left( \mathbf{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} - \mathbf{F}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} \right) - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y} \left( \mathbf{G}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} - \mathbf{G}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} \right) 
+ \Delta t \left[ (1 - C_{\text{im}}) \mathbf{K}_{ij}^{n} + C_{\text{im}} \mathbf{K}_{ij}^{n+1} \right],$$
(13)

where  $\Delta t = t^{n+1} - t^n$  is the time step and

$$\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{ij}^n = \frac{1}{|\Omega_{ij}|} \int_{\Omega_{ij}} \mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}, t^n) d\mathbf{x}, \quad \overline{\mathbf{U}}_{ij}^{n+1} = \frac{1}{|\Omega_{ij}|} \int_{\Omega_{ij}} \mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}, t^{n+1}) d\mathbf{x}.$$

The finite volume scheme (13) distinguishes from those developed solely based on Riemann solutions by two means: numerical fluxes are evaluated at  $t^{n+\frac{1}{2}} = t^n + \frac{1}{2}\Delta t$  and a semi-implicit time discretization to the source term is used.

The numerical flux across the cell interface  $\Gamma_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}=\{x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\}\times[y_{j-\frac{1}{2}},y_{j+\frac{1}{2}}]$  is approximated with second-order accuracy by

$$\mathbf{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}, t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})), \tag{14}$$

where  $\mathbf{x}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j} = (x_{i+\frac{1}{2}},y_j)$ . The cell interface value  $\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j},\cdot)$  at  $t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}$  is obtained by the Taylor expansion

$$\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j},t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}) = \mathbf{U}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*} + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{U}}{\partial t}\right)_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*}, \tag{15}$$

which is second-order accurate in time. The Riemann solution  $\mathbf{U}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*}$  and the instantaneous time derivative  $(\partial \mathbf{U}/\partial t)_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*}$  are obtained by solving the GRP of governing equations (10) at  $(\mathbf{x}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j},t^n)$ , which is formulated as the IVP

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{U}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial y} = \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{U}, \nabla \mathbf{U}),$$

$$\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}, t^n) = \begin{cases}
\mathbf{U}_{i,j}^n(\mathbf{x}), & x < x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}, \\
\mathbf{U}_{i+1,j}^n(\mathbf{x}), & x > x_{i+\frac{1}{2}},
\end{cases}$$
(16)

where  $\mathbf{x} = (x, y)$ . The piecewise smooth initial data  $\mathbf{U}_{i,j}^n(\mathbf{x})$  and  $\mathbf{U}_{i+1,j}^n(\mathbf{x})$  are obtained by the space data reconstruction procedure presented in Appendix A. The GRP solver to solve the IVP (16) will be developed in the next section. If a higher order of the spatial accuracy is desired, the numerical fluxes can be computed by the Gauss-Legendre integration and we do not elaborate tedious but basic computations. The numerical flux  $\mathbf{G}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}$  can be computed in the same manner.

The parameter  $C_{\text{im}} \in [0, 1]$  determines the time discretization to the source term in the volume fraction discretization. The source term **K** is approximated at  $t^n$  and  $t^{n+1}$  by

$$\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{n} = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, \overline{(\alpha_{1})}_{ij}^{n} K_{ij}^{n} \eta_{ij}^{n}]^{\top}, \quad \mathbf{K}_{ij}^{n+1} = [0, 0, 0, 0, \overline{(\alpha_{1})}_{ij}^{n+1} K_{ij}^{n+1} \eta_{ij}^{n+1}]^{\top},$$

where

$$\eta_{ij}^{n} = \frac{1}{|\Omega_{ij}|} \int_{\Omega_{ij}} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}|_{t=t^{n}} d\mathbf{x}, \quad \eta_{ij}^{n+1} = \frac{1}{|\Omega_{ij}|} \int_{\Omega_{ij}} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u}|_{t=t^{n+1}} d\mathbf{x}.$$
 (17)

The velocity divergence in (17) is approximated by the Gauss-Green formula, and details will be specified in the next subsection.

## B. Crank-Nicolson time evolution of the volume fraction equation

For the simplicity of the presentation, this subsection illustrates the Crank-Nicolson evolution to the volume fraction equation (9) in one space dimension. In order to further simplify notations, we drop the subscript referring to the phase index and denote the volume fraction of the phase k = 1 by  $\alpha$ .

For arbitrary  $C_{\text{im}} \in [0, 1]$ , the semi-implicit discretization to (9) in the cell  $I_i = [x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}, x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}]$  is

$$\bar{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1} = \bar{\alpha}_{i}^{n} - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left[ (u\alpha)_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} - (u\alpha)_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} \right]$$

$$+ (1 - C_{im}) \Delta t \bar{\alpha}_{i}^{n} K_{i}^{n} \eta_{i}^{n} + C_{im} \Delta t \bar{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1} K_{i}^{n+1} \eta_{i}^{n+1}.$$

$$(18)$$

By using the Remann solution of the velocity  $u_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*}$  and the Newton-Leibniz formula, the approximation to the divergence of the velocity field at  $t^n$  is

$$\eta_i^n = \frac{u_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*} - u_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*}}{\Delta x}.$$
(19)

Similarly,

$$\eta_i^{n+1} = \frac{\hat{u}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+1} - \hat{u}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+1}}{\Delta x},\tag{20}$$

where  $\hat{u}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+1} = u_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*} + \Delta t (\partial u/\partial t)_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*}$  is the estimation of u at  $(x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}, t^{n+1})$ .

Remark II.1. The time derivative  $(\partial u/\partial t)_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*}$  allows one to evolve the solution at cell interfaces. As a result, the velocity divergence at  $t^{n+1}$  can be approximated in a straightforward way (20). This is not available for finite volume schemes solely based on Riemann solutions.

Remark II.2. In two space dimensions, the Gauss-Green formula is used. Therefore, the approximation to the divergence of the velocity field in a polygonal cell  $\Omega_J$  at  $t^n$  is

$$\eta_J^n = \frac{1}{|\Omega_J|} \sum_{\{\Gamma\}} |\Gamma| \Big( \sum_g^G w_g \mathbf{u}_{\Gamma,g}^{n,*} \cdot \mathbf{n}_{\Gamma} \Big), \tag{21}$$

where  $\{\Gamma\}$  is the set of all edges of  $\Omega_J$ ,  $\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}$  is the unit outer normal of  $\Gamma$ ,  $\mathbf{u}_{\Gamma,g}^{n,*}$  is the Riemann solution of  $\mathbf{u}$  at the g-th Gauss quadrature point on  $\Gamma$ , and  $w_g$  is the corresponding quadrature weight. In particular, given a rectangular cell  $\Omega_{ij}$ , we obtain the second-order accurate approximation:

$$\eta_{ij}^{n} = \frac{u_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*} - u_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*}}{\Delta x} + \frac{v_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*} - v_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*}}{\Delta y}.$$
 (22)

The divergence approximation  $\eta_{ij}^{n+1}$  can be obtained in the same way by using  $\hat{u}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+1}$  and  $\hat{v}_{i,j\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{n+1}$ .

By taking  $C_{\text{im}} = \frac{1}{2}$ , the Crank-Nicolson discretization of the volume fraction equation is obtained. In such a case, rewrite (18) as

$$\bar{\alpha}_i^{n+1} = \tilde{\alpha}_i^n + \theta \bar{\alpha}_i^{n+1} K_i^{n+1}, \tag{23}$$

where  $\theta = \frac{\Delta t}{2} \eta_i^{n+1}$  and

$$\tilde{\alpha}_i^n \triangleq \bar{\alpha}_i^n - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left[ \left( u\alpha \right)_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} - \left( u\alpha \right)_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}} \right] + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \bar{\alpha}_i^n K_i^n \eta_i^n. \tag{24}$$

In the case of stiffened gas EOS, (12) yields

$$K_i^{n+1} = \frac{\gamma_2(p_i^{n+1} + \pi_2)}{\bar{\alpha}_i^{n+1}\gamma_2(p_i^{n+1} + \pi_2) + \left[1 - \bar{\alpha}_i^{n+1}\right]\gamma_1(p_i^{n+1} + \pi_1)}.$$
 (25)

Here the pressure  $p_i^{n+1}$  is determined by the EOS of the mixture, which depends on  $\bar{\alpha}_i^{n+1}$ . Given the internal energy  $\overline{(\rho e)}_i^{n+1}$ , which is obtained by the finite volume scheme (13), the pressure is determined by the relation

$$\overline{(\rho e)}_{i}^{n+1} = \overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1} \overline{(\rho_{1} e_{1})}_{i}^{n+1} + (1 - \overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1}) \overline{(\rho_{2} e_{2})}_{i}^{n+1}$$

$$= \frac{\overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1} (p_{i}^{n+1} + \gamma_{1} \pi_{1})}{\gamma_{1} - 1} + \frac{(1 - \overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1}) (p_{i}^{n+1} + \gamma_{2} \pi_{2})}{\gamma_{2} - 1}.$$

Therefore, the pressure  $p_i^{n+1}$  depends on the volume fraction  $\bar{\alpha}_i^{n+1}$  through

$$p_{i}^{n+1} = \frac{(\gamma_{1} - 1)(\gamma_{2} - 1)\overline{(\rho e)_{i}^{n+1}} - \gamma_{1}\pi_{1}(\gamma_{2} - 1)\overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1} - \gamma_{2}\pi_{2}(\gamma_{1} - 1)\left[1 - \overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1}\right]}{(\gamma_{2} - \gamma_{1})\overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1} + \gamma_{1} - 1}$$

$$\triangleq \frac{L_{1}\left(\overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1}\right)}{L_{2}\left(\overline{\alpha}_{i}^{n+1}\right)}.$$
(26)

Substituting (24), (25), and (26) into (23) derives a semi-implicit evolution of the volume fraction equation (9), which is written as the nonlinear equation

$$f\left(\bar{\alpha}_{j}^{n+1}; \tilde{\alpha}_{j}^{n}\right) = 0. \tag{27}$$

The left hand side is a rational function defined as

$$f(\xi; \tilde{\alpha}) = \xi - \tilde{\alpha} - \frac{\theta \left[ \gamma_2 L_1(\xi) + \gamma_2 \pi_2 L_2(\xi) \right] \xi}{\left[ (\gamma_2 - \gamma_1) \xi + \gamma_1 \right] L_1(\xi) + \left[ (\gamma_2 \pi_2 - \gamma_1 \pi_1) \xi + \gamma_1 \pi_1 \right] L_2(\xi)}, \tag{28}$$

with  $\xi$  being the unknown. The Newton-Raphson iteration can be used to solve (27).

## C. Bound preserving of the volume fraction

It is trivial to check that f defined in (28) is monotonically decreasing with respect to  $\tilde{\alpha}$  and monotonically increasing with respect to  $\xi$ , provided that  $(\xi, \tilde{\alpha}) \in [0, 1] \times [0, 1 - \theta]$ . In addition,  $f(0;0) = f(1;1-\theta) = 0$ . So the necessary and sufficient condition for the nonlinear equation  $f(\xi;\tilde{\alpha}) = 0$  possessing a unique solution in the interval  $\xi \in [0,1]$  is that  $0 \le \tilde{\alpha} \le 1 - \theta$ . In what follows, a restriction on the time step  $\Delta t$  is established to ensure the bound-preserving condition  $0 \le \tilde{\alpha} \le 1 - \theta$ .

By dropping the subscript i and the superscript n indicating the cell index and the time step, simplify notations in (24) and rewrite it as

$$\tilde{\alpha} = \bar{\alpha} - \Delta t \left( \beta + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \beta_t \right) + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \bar{\alpha} \frac{\omega - 1}{\omega - \bar{\alpha}} \eta, \tag{29}$$

where

$$\beta = \frac{(u\alpha)_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*} - (u\alpha)_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*}}{\Delta x}, \quad \beta_t = \frac{[(u\alpha)_t]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*} - [(u\alpha)_t]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n,*}}{\Delta x}$$

and

$$\omega = \frac{\rho_1 c_1^2}{\rho_1 c_1^2 - \rho_2 c_2^2}. (30)$$

From (29), the inequality  $\tilde{\alpha} \geq 0$  derives

$$\frac{\beta_t}{2}\Delta t^2 + \left(\beta - \frac{\bar{\alpha}}{2}\frac{\omega - 1}{\omega - \bar{\alpha}}\eta\right)\Delta t \le \bar{\alpha}.\tag{31}$$

By basic calculations, the inequality  $\tilde{\alpha} \leq 1 - \theta$  derives

$$\frac{\hat{\beta}_t}{2}\Delta t^2 + \left(\hat{\beta} - \frac{\hat{\alpha}}{2}\frac{\hat{\omega} - 1}{\hat{\omega} - \hat{\alpha}}\eta\right)\Delta t \le \hat{\alpha},\tag{32}$$

where βˆ = η − β, βˆ <sup>t</sup> = η<sup>t</sup> − β<sup>t</sup> , ˆα = 1 − α, and ˆω = 1 − ω. The two inequalities can be treated in the same way since they have the same form.

Take (31) for example, whose left hand side is a quadratic function of ∆t. It is easy to find a ∆t<sup>0</sup> such that (31) holds for ∀∆t ≤ ∆t0. Therefore, besides the CFL condition, the time step ∆t is additionally restricted due to inequality (31). However, ∆t<sup>0</sup> approaches 0 as ¯α → 0 <sup>+</sup> if β − α¯ 2 ω−1 ω−α¯ η > 0. So a cut-off treatment is employed here, which means that in practical computations, the additional time step restriction according to (31) is only applied in cells where ¯α > 10<sup>−</sup><sup>6</sup> . Similarly, the time step is also restricted according to inequality (32) in cells where ¯α < 1 − 10<sup>−</sup><sup>6</sup> .

## III. ACOUSTIC GENERALIZED RIEMANN PROBLEM SOLVER

The objective of this section is to develop the GRP solver for the Kapila model under the acoustic assumption, which serves as the building block of the finite volume scheme proposed in the previous section. There are two versions of the GRP solver: the acoustic version and the nonlinear version. The acoustic solver, which linearizes the governing equations, is suitable when the waves emerging from the initial discontinuity are relatively weak30,48. Conversely, in the presence of strong waves, the nonlinear GRP solver is required. The development of the nonlinear GRP solver for the Kapila model is left for a future investigation<sup>10</sup>. To make the present paper self-containing, we develop the GRP solver under the acoustic assumption in this section.

By setting t <sup>n</sup> = 0 and shifting xi<sup>+</sup> <sup>1</sup> 2 ,j to the origin x<sup>0</sup> = (0, 0), the GRP (16) becomes

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{U}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial y} = \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{U}, \nabla \mathbf{U}),$$

$$\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}, t = 0) = \begin{cases}
\mathbf{U}_{L}(\mathbf{x}), & x < 0, \\
\mathbf{U}_{R}(\mathbf{x}), & x > 0.
\end{cases}$$
(33)

In finite volume schemes, the piecewise smooth initial data are obtained by the space data reconstruction procedure in cells that are adjacent to the initial discontinuity {x : x = 0}.

#### A. Solve the associated Riemann problem

Following Ref. 7, the first step of solving the GRP (33) is to solve the associated Riemann problem

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{U}^{\text{ass}}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U}^{\text{ass}})}{\partial x} = \mathbf{K}^{\text{ass}}(\mathbf{U}^{\text{ass}}, \nabla \mathbf{U}^{\text{ass}}),$$

$$\mathbf{U}^{\text{ass}}(\mathbf{x}, t = 0) = \begin{cases}
\mathbf{U}_{L}^{\text{ass}} = \mathbf{U}_{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0}), & x < 0, \\
\mathbf{U}_{R}^{\text{ass}} = \mathbf{U}_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{0}), & x > 0,
\end{cases}$$
(34)

where

$$\mathbf{K}^{\mathrm{ass}} = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, \alpha_1 K \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}]^{\top}.$$

A concern that arises in this situation is the ambiguity of the Hugoniot relation determining the shock behavior, which is a result of the governing equations being genuinely non-conservative. Here we use the one proposed in Ref. 41,

$$\zeta_{k} = \zeta_{k}^{0}, 
\rho(u - \sigma) = \rho^{0}(u^{0} - \sigma) \triangleq m, 
p - p^{0} = m^{2}(\tau - \tau^{0}), \ne_{k} - e_{k}^{0} + \frac{p + p^{0}}{2}(\tau_{k} - \tau_{k}^{0}) = 0,$$
(35)

where  $\sigma$  is the shock speed,  $\tau = \frac{1}{\rho}$  is the specific volume of the mixture, and  $q^0$  denotes the pre-shock value of a certain physical quantity q. The advantages of the Hugoniot relation (35) are highlighted as follows<sup>34,41</sup>: (i) The conservation of the total energy is preserved. (ii) It resembles to single-phase shock relations. (iii) It preserves the positivity of volume fractions. (iv) It is symmetric with respect to all phases. (v) It is tangent to the mixture isentrope, which is important for the well-posedness of the Riemann problem. (vi) It agrees with a variety of experimental measurements.

Based on Hugoniot relation (35), the IVP (34) can be solved analytically by the exact Riemann solver<sup>34</sup>. The Riemann solution is therefore obtained and denoted as

$$\mathbf{U}^* = R^A(0, \mathbf{U}_L^{\text{ass}}, \mathbf{U}_R^{\text{ass}}) = \lim_{t \to 0^+} \mathbf{U}^{\text{ass}}(\mathbf{x}_0, t). \tag{36}$$

Remark III.1. Note that the initial data used in (34) are a piecewise constant functions by evaluating  $\mathbf{U}_{L/R}$  at  $\mathbf{x}_0$ , which means that the spatial variation of the flow field is neglected in numerical computations if the scheme is developed solely based on the Riemann solution.

Moreover, by considering  $\mathbf{K}^{\mathrm{ass}}$  instead of  $\mathbf{K}$  as the source term, only the normal component (the x-derivative) of the velocity divergence is taken into account and the transversal component is dropped. Therefore, the compaction or expansion effect of the flow field is not fully included in the Riemann solution.

Remark III.2. The ambiguity of the Hugoniot relation also arises at the discrete level, since numerical solutions of non-conservative hyperbolic PDEs depend on the way to discretize governing equations. Nevertheless, the numerical experiments conducted in Section IV indicate that the treatment employed by the finite volume scheme (13) produces numerical results that agree with exact solutions determined by the Hugoniot relation (35).

#### B. Solve the acoustic generalized Riemann problem

Regarding primitive variables as unknowns, rewrite the governing equations in the quasilinear form

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{A} \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial x} + \mathbf{B} \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial y} = 0, \tag{37a}$$

$$\frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial t} + u \frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial x} + v \frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial y} = (K - \alpha_1) \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \right), \tag{37b}$$

where

$$\mathbf{V} = \begin{bmatrix} \zeta_1 \\ \rho \\ u \\ v \\ p \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} u & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & u & \rho & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & u & 0 & \frac{1}{\rho} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & u & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \rho c^2 & 0 & u \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} v & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & v & 0 & \rho & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & v & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & v & \frac{1}{\rho} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \rho c^2 & v \end{bmatrix}.$$
(38)

By regarding (37) as governing equations, the IVP (33) becomes

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{A} \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial x} + \mathbf{B} \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial y} = 0,$$

$$\frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial t} + u \frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial x} + v \frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial y} = (K - \alpha_1) \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \right),$$

$$\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x}, t = 0) = \begin{cases}
\mathbf{V}_L(\mathbf{x}), & x < 0, \\
\mathbf{V}_R(\mathbf{x}), & x > 0, \\
(\alpha_1)_L(\mathbf{x}), & x < 0, \\
(\alpha_1)_R(\mathbf{x}), & x > 0.
\end{cases}$$
(39)

The initial values of  $\mathbf{V}$  and  $\alpha_1$  are obtained by transforming the reconstructed data of  $\mathbf{U}$  into those of primitive variables. By noting that (37a) is independent of (37b), the acoustic GRP solver<sup>7</sup> can be directly applied to solve the IVP of (37a).

With the Riemann solution  $\mathbf{U}^*$  obtained in (36), fix the coefficient matrices as  $\mathbf{A}^* = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{U}^*)$  and  $\mathbf{B}^* = \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{U}^*)$ . By the characteristic decomposition,

$$\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{V}}{\partial t}\right)^{*} = -\mathbf{R}^{*} \mathbf{\Lambda}_{+}^{*} \mathbf{L}^{*} \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}_{L}}{\partial x} \right|_{\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_{0}} - \mathbf{R}^{*} \mathbf{\Lambda}_{-}^{*} \mathbf{L}^{*} \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}_{R}}{\partial x} \right|_{\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_{0}} - \mathbf{R}^{*} \mathbf{I}_{+}^{*} \mathbf{L}^{*} \mathbf{B}^{*} \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}_{L}}{\partial y} \right|_{\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_{0}} - \mathbf{R}^{*} \mathbf{I}_{-}^{*} \mathbf{L}^{*} \mathbf{B}^{*} \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{V}_{R}}{\partial y} \right|_{\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_{0}}.$$
(40)

The matrices  $\mathbf{R}^*$ ,  $\mathbf{L}^*$  and  $\mathbf{\Lambda}^*$  are given by the decomposition of  $\mathbf{A}^*$  as

$$\mathbf{A}^* = \mathbf{R}^* \mathbf{\Lambda}^* \mathbf{L}^*.$$

where  $L^*$  is the inverse of  $R^*$ . The matrix  $R = [R_1, R_2, R_3, R_4, R_5]$  is composed of the right eigenvectors of A, which are

$$\mathbf{R}_{1} = [0, \frac{1}{c^{2}}, -\frac{1}{\rho c}, 0, 1]^{\top}, \quad \mathbf{R}_{2} = [0, 0, 0, 1, 0]^{\top}, \quad \mathbf{R}_{3} = [0, 1, 0, 0, 0]^{\top},$$

$$\mathbf{R}_{4} = [1, 0, 0, v, \frac{1}{\rho}]^{\top}, \quad \mathbf{R}_{5} = [0, \frac{1}{c^{2}}, \frac{1}{\rho c}, 0, 1]^{\top}.$$

Corresponding eigenvalues of **A** are  $\lambda_1 = u - c$ ,  $\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = \lambda_4 = u$ , and  $\lambda_5 = u + c$ . Furthermore,

$$\mathbf{\Lambda}_{+} = \operatorname{diag}\{\max(\lambda_{i}, 0)\}, \quad \mathbf{\Lambda}_{-} = \operatorname{diag}\{\min(\lambda_{i}, 0)\}, 
\mathbf{I}_{+} = \operatorname{diag}\{\max(\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_{i}), 0)\}, \quad \mathbf{I}_{-} = \operatorname{diag}\{\max(\operatorname{sign}(-\lambda_{i}), 0)\}.$$

The last step is to compute the time derivative of the volume fraction  $\alpha_1$ . Instead of directly solving the IVP of (37b), in what follows, we derive the value of  $(\partial \alpha_1/\partial t)^*$  from  $(\partial \mathbf{V}/\partial t)^*$  obtained in (40). The time derivative of the phase density is

$$\frac{\partial \rho_1}{\partial t} = \left[ \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho_1} \right)_{s_1} \right]^{-1} \left[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} - \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial s_1} \right)_{\rho_1} \frac{\partial s_1}{\partial t} \right] 
= \frac{1}{c_1^2} \left[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + u \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial s_1} \right)_{\rho_1} \frac{\partial s_1}{\partial x} \right].$$

Multiplying the above equation by  $\tau_1$  and taking the limit  $t \to 0^+$  lead to

$$\left(\tau_1 \frac{\partial \rho_1}{\partial t}\right)^* = \frac{1}{\rho_1^* c_1^{*2}} \left[ \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\right)^* + u^* \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial s_1}\right)_{\rho_1}^* \left(\frac{\partial s_1}{\partial x}\right)^* \right],\tag{41}$$

where  $(\frac{\partial p}{\partial t})^*$  is obtained in (40). For stiffened gases,  $\rho_1^* c_1^{*2} = \gamma_1(p^* + \pi_1)$ . By the fact that  $\alpha_1 \tau = \zeta_1 \tau_1$ ,

$$\begin{split} \frac{1}{\tau}d\tau + \frac{1}{\alpha_k}d\alpha_k &= \frac{1}{\tau_k}d\tau_k + \frac{1}{\zeta_k}d\zeta_k \\ &= -\tau_k d\rho_k + \frac{1}{\zeta_k}d\zeta_k. \end{split}$$

Therefore, by approaching the limit  $t \to 0^+$ .

$$\left(\frac{\partial \alpha_1}{\partial t}\right)^* = \alpha_1^* \left(-\tau_1 \frac{\partial \rho_1}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{\zeta_1} \frac{\partial \zeta_1}{\partial t} + \tau \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}\right)^*.$$
(42)

The acoustic GRP solver is finalized.

Remark III.3. It is illustrated by Lei and Li<sup>25</sup> that the GRP solver improves corresponding numerical schemes to effectively resolve multi-dimensional effects of the flow field. This advantage is of particular importance for the current scheme developed for the Kapila model, since the compaction or expansion effect, which is indeed a multi-dimensional effect, plays a key role in the volume fraction equation. By substituting governing equations (1) into (42), the time derivative of  $\alpha_1$  is expressed as

$$\left(\frac{\partial \alpha_{1}}{\partial t}\right)^{*} = \lim_{t \to 0^{+}} \frac{\partial \alpha_{1}}{\partial t} \Big|_{(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}, t)}$$

$$= \lim_{t \to 0^{+}} \left[ -u \frac{\partial \alpha_{1}}{\partial x} - v \frac{\partial \alpha_{1}}{\partial y} + (K - \alpha_{1}) \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \right) \right] \Big|_{(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}, t)}, \tag{43}$$

which is the Lax-Wendroff procedure applied to the volume fraction equation (1e). Compared with the Riemann solution obtained by solving the associated Riemann problem (34), the divergence of the velocity field, which reflects the compaction or expansion effect of the flow field, is fully incorporated into the time derivative (42).

## V. CONCLUSIONS

The Kapila model, derived from the seven-equation model by asymptotic analysis, is widely used in numerical simulations of compressible multiphase flows involving both fluid mixtures and pure fluids22,31,33,34,39,47. By adopting the GRP solver, the present paper contributes to the development of a second-order accurate finite volume scheme for the Kapila model, within the GRP framework27,28. Given the challenges posed by the stiffness of the Kapila model, the proposed scheme enhances the robustness through two key improvements: a semi-implicit time discretization to the stiff source term and the use of GRP-based numerical fluxes. The robustness of the current scheme is demonstrated by several challenging test cases involving either interfaces exhibiting large pressure and density ratios or multiphase fluid mixtures. A nonlinear multiphase flow test case with a smooth solution is proposed to verify the numerical accuracy.

It is worth mentioning that in the presence of strong nonlinear waves, the nonlinear GRP solver is imperative since it effectively resolves essential thermodynamical information regarding the evolution of the flow field, as demonstrated in the shock tube problem of Example 2 in Section IV. To keep the conciseness of this paper, we left this topic to a separate study Ref. 10.

To complete the finite volume scheme, the linear data reconstruction employed in Refs. 6 and 7 is adopted. Here we apply the linear reconstruction to primitive variables  $\mathbf{W} = [\zeta_1, \rho, u, v, p, \alpha_1]^{\top}$ .

In two space dimensions, **W** is linearly reconstructed in cell  $\Omega_{ij}$  as

$$\mathbf{W}_{i,j}^{n+1}(\mathbf{x}) = \widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i,j}^{n+1} + (\nabla \mathbf{W})_{i,j}^{n+1} \cdot (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_{i,j}). \tag{A1}$$

The cell centered value  $\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i,j}^{n+1}$  is computed using cell average values  $\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{i,j}^{n+1}$  obtained in (13). The approximation to the slope is

$$\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{W}}{\partial x}\right)_{i,j}^{n+1} = \operatorname{minmod}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i,j}^{n+1} - \widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i-1,j}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}, \kappa \frac{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+1} - \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}, \frac{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+1} - \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i,j}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}\right), \quad (A2)$$

where the cell interface values are computed as

$$\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+1} = \sum_{g} \omega_{g} \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+1,g}, \quad \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n+1} = \mathbf{W}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*,g} + \left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{W}}{\partial t}\right)_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n,*,g}.$$
(A3)

The coefficient  $\kappa \in [0,2)$  is a user-tuned parameter. The minmod function is defined as

$$\operatorname{minmod}(a, b, c) = \begin{cases} \operatorname{sign}(a) \operatorname{min}(|a|, |b|, |c|), & \text{if } \operatorname{sign}(a) = \operatorname{sign}(b) = \operatorname{sign}(c), \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
(A4)

The other component  $(\partial \mathbf{W}/\partial y)_{i,j}^{n+1}$  of the gradient can be computed similarly. By transforming the reconstructed piecewise linear function of  $\mathbf{W}$  into those of  $\mathbf{U}$ , the initial value to be used in the IVP (33) is obtained.

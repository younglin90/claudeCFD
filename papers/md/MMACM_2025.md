#### **ABSTRACT**

The five-equation model is commonly utilized for simulating compressible multiphase flows. Conventional high-resolution schemes, however, frequently suffer from excessive numerical dissipation that leads to interface smearing, thereby compromising simulation accuracy in interfacial flow characterization. In this work, we propose an improved multi-material artificial compression method with extreme interface sharpening (MMACM-Ex), a novel high-fidelity numerical method specifically designed for solving the five-equation model. The method discretizes the equations within a standard finite volume framework and calculates the numerical fluxes with two components: an upwind Riemann flux derived through the Harten-Lax van Leer contact (HLLC) Riemann solver, combined with an interface sharpening flux formulated using a downwind scheme adapted from the flux-corrected transport volume-of-fluid (FCT-VOF) method. Besides, a characteristic function is strategically designed to ensure that the interface sharpening flux can effectively counteract numerical dissipation generated by Riemann flux computations while preserving rigorous interface sharpness. The proposed numerical method is versatile and applicable to a wide range of one-, two-, and three-dimensional problems. Numerical validations demonstrate the method's ability to capture material interfaces with high resolution, highlighting its superiority over the original MMACM in simulating compressible multiphase flows.

#### I. INTRODUCTION

Compressible multiphase fluid dynamics is a vibrant and challenging research area of significant importance in many industrial applications. Within this domain, the intricate interplay between interfaces and surrounding flows, including shocks, plays a crucial role in interface dynamics.<sup>2</sup> As the presence of material interfaces significantly complicates the underlying physics, rendering problems notably arduous for both analytical and experimental approaches in many cases, it is necessary to employ direct numerical simulations to study the temporal evolution of flow variables and the fundamental mechanisms behind the flows. In this context, given the critical importance of accurately resolving small-scale interfacial flows, it is paramount to sustain the sharpness of the interface throughout the simulations.

To address the challenges posed by multiphase compressible flows, numerous numerical approaches have been developed, which can be categorized into two groups: the sharp interface method (SIM) and the diffuse interface method (DIM). In SIM frameworks, fluid interfaces are mathematically represented as discontinuous jumps in physical quantities. In contrast, DIM formulations introduce an artificial mixing zone between distinct phases, which emerge naturally through numerical dissipation mechanisms within the computational

SIM includes arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) methods,<sup>3,4</sup> Front-tracking methods, 5-7 and level-set/ghost fluid methods. 8-11 By preserving material interfaces as discontinuities, these approaches have demonstrated efficacy for compressible multiphase flow modeling. However, they encounter significant limitations when modeling severe interfacial deformations involving topological changes, as exemplified by ALE and front-tracking implementations. 12 Level-set methods, while circumventing explicit interface tracking through implicit signed distance function representations, suffer from inherent conservation deficiencies. To address this issue, Hu et al. 13 introduced the cut-cell method to establish a fully conservative framework for compressible flows, wherein structured cells are partitioned by interfaces into unstructured subcells, followed by solving local Riemann problems at cell faces to determine interfacial fluxes. Although recent advances

<sup>&</sup>lt;sup>a)</sup>Author to whom correspondence should be addressed: he\_zhiwei@iapcm.ac.cn

have enhanced robustness during topological transitions <sup>14–17</sup> and extended applicability to diverse flow regimes, <sup>18–20</sup> they still face challenges in computational efficiency and implementation complexity, which impede their engineering applications.

Compared to SIM, DIM offers a more straightforward implementation to deal with complicated interface morphology. Within this category, the five-equation model, 21-24 governing mass conservation for each phase, momentum, and energy conservation for the mixture, and the advection of volume fraction, has been derived based on defining the mixture of materials in cells intersected by interfaces. This model is a simplification of the more intricate seven-equation model introduced by Baer and Nunziato,<sup>25</sup> assuming that the velocities and pressures of each material reach equilibrium in a shorter timescale compared to other characteristic timescales of the flow. As the system guarantees the property of discrete conservation inherently by using standard finite volume methods, the fiveequation models have attracted increasing attention and have been extensively utilized in the literature. In addition, extensive studies have delved into various physical phenomena within this framework, including viscous effects, surface tension,<sup>26</sup> phase transition,<sup>27</sup> elastic/plastic materials,<sup>28</sup> and deflagration and detonation.<sup>29</sup> Further exploration and detailed information on these topics can be found in review papers provided therein. 12,30

However, particular attention must be paid when computing the volume fraction for compressible multiphase flows due to the intrinsic numerical dissipation in conventional Eulerian advection schemes. This numerical dissipation tends to blur out the jumps in volume fraction functions that are crucial for identifying material interfaces between different fluids. In the case of shock waves, the convergence of characteristics acts as a counteraction to the numerical diffusion; therefore, shocks in compressible flows generally "want" to remain sharp, and slight smoothing of the shock is immediately repaired. In contrast, there is no inherent mechanism that sharpens a smoothed interface (contact discontinuity), and the flow does not "know" the difference between an absolutely sharp interface and a smoother one. Thus, artificial smearing of the function that represents the interface is inevitable unless constraints are applied to the governing equations. While higher-order schemes such as the weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO) scheme<sup>31–34</sup> can largely reduce numerical dissipation and improve accuracy,<sup>35–37</sup> they may introduce numerical oscillations in compressible multiphase flows with complex equations of state (EOS),36 and the fluid interface can still diffuse in a long period of evolution.

To keep the compact thickness of material interfaces during computation, various interface-sharpening techniques have been proposed to steepen the jumps in the volume fraction fields. Shukla, Pantano, and Freund<sup>38</sup> proposed the interface-compression method for solving the volume fraction and phase densities, in which extra correction terms are added in the corresponding equations to eliminate the nonphysical spread of the interface. Subsequent works by Tiwari, Freund, and Pantano<sup>39</sup> and Shukla<sup>40</sup> further refined this method to reduce conservation errors. On the other hand, So, Hu, and Adams<sup>41</sup> introduced an anti-diffusion strategy implementing sharpening effects via post-processing solutions of an anti-diffusion equation.

An alternative approach emerges through the tangent of hyperbola for interface capturing (THINC) scheme, originally formulated by Xiao, Honma, and Kono. <sup>42</sup> This scheme leverages the characteristics of the hyperbolic tangent function to mimic the jump-like profile of the volume fraction field, enabling accurate capture of sharp interfaces in a simple and efficient manner. Shyue and Xiao <sup>43</sup> integrated THINC with the monotonic upstream-center scheme for conservation laws (MUSCL) for compressible multi-component flows. Deng *et al.* <sup>44</sup> introduced a boundary variation diminishing (BVD) framework <sup>45</sup> to minimize the variations of the reconstructed variables at cell boundaries, thereby effectively reducing the dissipation error in numerical solutions. Recent work by Zhang *et al.* <sup>46</sup> coupled the THINC method with the WENO5IS scheme, <sup>34</sup> while Takagi *et al.*, <sup>47</sup> Liang *et al.*, <sup>48</sup> and Li and Fu<sup>49</sup> combined THINC with targeted essentially non-oscillatory (TENO) schemes, <sup>50</sup> achieving high-resolution capture of both shock waves and material interfaces simultaneously.

More recently, He and Tan<sup>51</sup> established a comprehensive theoretical framework for interface sharpening within the five-equation model, which preserves the conservation properties of physical quantities while maintaining asymptotic compatibility with the thermodynamic mixture laws. Building upon this foundation, they proposed the multi-material artificial compression method (MMACM) to numerically implement the formulation by introducing a consistent set of interface-sharpening fluxes for the mass, momentum, total energy, and volume fraction equations. However, a critical aspect of the original MMACM formulation requires further development: the expression of volume fraction flux accounting for sharpening effects lacks in-depth analysis and is temporarily approximated using the steepnessadjustable harmonic limiter.<sup>52</sup> Consequently, despite demonstrated interface-sharpening efficacy, MMACM simulations exhibit residual numerical dissipation artifacts, which could potentially be mitigated by redesigned volume fraction flux expressions. Furthermore, the framework's applicability to complex EOS, particularly under aggressive interface-sharpening conditions, remains unexplored and presents a significant avenue for future research.

In this study, we develop the MMACM-Ex, an enhanced version of the original MMACM, which can sharpen interfaces extremely within two grid widths. Within this new method, the volume fraction flux that accounts for the sharpening effect is reformulated with a downwind scheme adapted from the flux-corrected transport volume-of-fluid (FCT-VOF) method.<sup>53</sup> Additionally, the scheme is proven not to introduce numerical instabilities through a strategically designed characteristic function. Numerical tests have been conducted to validate the effectiveness of the proposed method in accurately capturing material interfaces as sharp jumps in volume fraction. Furthermore, the method is also applied to the complex EOS for different materials, demonstrating its versatility and robustness in simulating compressible multiphase flows.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, the governing equations of the five-equation model for multiphase flow are described. In Sec. III, the details of the method are presented. In Sec. IV, numerical results of benchmark tests are provided in comparison with the original MMACM. Finally, the conclusions are summarized in Sec. V.

#### II. MATHEMATICAL MODEL

#### A. Governing equations

In the present work, the inviscid compressible multiphase flows are formulated by the five-equation model developed by Allaire, Clerc, and Kokh.<sup>22</sup> We consider two phases in the flow region; thus, the model consists of two continuity equations for each phase, a momentum equation, a total energy equation, and an advection equation for one of the volume fractions as follows:

$$\begin{cases} \frac{\partial \alpha_{1} \rho_{1}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\alpha_{1} \rho_{1} \boldsymbol{u}) = 0, \\ \frac{\partial \alpha_{2} \rho_{2}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\alpha_{2} \rho_{2} \boldsymbol{u}) = 0, \\ \frac{\partial \rho \boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \boldsymbol{u} \boldsymbol{u}) + \nabla p = 0, \\ \frac{\partial \rho \boldsymbol{E}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \boldsymbol{E} \boldsymbol{u}) + \nabla \cdot (p \boldsymbol{u}) = 0, \\ \frac{\partial \alpha_{1}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\alpha_{1} \boldsymbol{u}) = \alpha_{1} \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u}, \end{cases}$$
(1)

where  $\rho_k$  (k=1,2) and  $\alpha_k$  denote the phasic density (kg/m³) and volume fraction of the kth phase, while  $\rho$ , u, p are the mixture density (kg/m³), velocity (m/s), and pressure (N/m²), respectively. Besides, E stands for the total energy, where  $\rho E = \rho e + \frac{1}{2} \rho u \cdot u$ , with e representing the internal energy. When considering more than two phases, the five-equation model can be extended by supplementing additional continuity equations and volume fraction advection equations for each new phase.

In this article, the Mie–Grüneisen EOS is used for each phase to close the above five-equation system, which can be employed to approximate a wide variety of materials including some gaseous or solid explosives and solid metals under high pressure

$$p_k = P_{\infty,k}(\rho_k) + \rho_k \Gamma_k(\rho_k) (e_k - e_{\infty,k}(\rho_k)), \tag{2}$$

where  $e_k$  is the phasic internal energy of kth phase,  $\Gamma_k = (1/\rho_k)(\partial p_k/\partial e_k)|_{\rho_k}$  is the Grüneisen coefficient, and  $P_{\infty,k}$ ,  $e_{\infty,k}$  are the properly chosen states of the pressure and internal energy along some reference curve to match the experimental data of the material being examined. Usually, the parameters  $\Gamma_k$ ,  $P_{\infty,k}$ , and  $e_{\infty,k}$  can be taken as functions only of the density, which makes the Mie-Grüneisen form [Eq. (2)] widely used.

#### **B.** Mixture rules

In the interfacial region, an artificial mixing zone inherently arises between immiscible fluids due to numerical dissipation in the computational scheme. To characterize effective material properties within this region, the following mixing constraints are needed for the five-equation system to ensure thermodynamic consistency:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{2} \alpha_k = 1,\tag{3}$$

$$\sum_{k=1}^{2} \alpha_k \rho_k = \rho, \tag{4}$$

$$\sum_{k=1}^{2} \alpha_k \rho_k e_k = \rho e. \tag{5}$$

Considering the isobaric assumption,  $^{22}$  i.e.,  $p_1 = p_2 = p$ , the mixture pressure can be calculated according to the mixture rules Eqs. (3)–(5) as follows:

$$p = \left(\sum_{k=1}^{2} \frac{\alpha_k P_{\infty,k}(\rho_k)}{\Gamma_k(\rho_k)} + \rho e - \sum_{k=1}^{2} \alpha_k \rho_k e_{\infty,k}(\rho_k)\right) / \sum_{k=1}^{2} \frac{\alpha_k}{\Gamma_k(\rho_k)}.$$
 (6)

The corresponding mixture sound speed c of this five-equation model could be computed as some kind of average of the phasic sound speeds  $c_k$  as

$$\rho c^{2} \left( \frac{\partial \rho e}{\partial p} \right)_{\rho} = \sum_{k=1}^{2} \alpha_{k} \left( \frac{\partial \rho_{k} e_{k}}{\partial p_{k}} \right)_{\rho_{k}} \rho_{k} c_{k}^{2}, \tag{7}$$

where

$$\left(\frac{\partial \rho e}{\partial p}\right)_{\rho} = \sum_{k=1}^{2} \alpha_{k} \left(\frac{\partial \rho_{k} e_{k}}{\partial p_{k}}\right)_{\rho_{k}},\tag{8}$$

$$c_k^2 = \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho_k} \Big|_{s_k} = \frac{p}{\rho_k^2} \frac{\partial p}{\partial e_k} \Big|_{\rho_k} + \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho_k} \Big|_{e_k}. \tag{9}$$

The above definition constitutes the general formulation for the five-equation model with isobaric closure. <sup>22,24</sup> Specifically, for the commonly used stiffened gas EOS given by  $p_k = (\gamma_k - 1)\rho_k e_k - \gamma_k p_{\infty,k}$ , where  $\gamma_k$  denotes the specific heat ratio and  $p_{\infty,k}$  is a material-dependent EOS parameter, the sound speed of the mixture [Eq. (7)] can be reformulated as

$$c = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma(p + p_{\infty})}{\rho}},\tag{10}$$

where the mixture parameters  $\gamma$  and  $p_{\infty}$  are calculated with the following relations:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{2} \frac{\alpha_k}{\gamma_k - 1} = \frac{1}{\gamma - 1},\tag{11}$$

$$\sum_{k=1}^{2} \frac{\alpha_k \gamma_k p_{\infty,k}}{\gamma_k - 1} = \frac{\gamma p_\infty}{\gamma - 1}.$$
 (12)

#### III. NUMERICAL METHOD

The five-equation model [Eq. (1)] in quasi-conservative form can be discretized by a Godunov-type scheme. After integrating the governing equations over a small control volume  $V_{i,j,k} = \Delta x \Delta y \Delta z$ , where i, j, and k are the indices of the cell in the x-, y-, and z-direction, respectively, the semi-discrete form in three dimension for cell (i, j, k) is given by

$$\frac{d\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{i,j,k}}{dt} = \frac{1}{\Delta x} \left( \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{x,i-1/2,j,k} - \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{x,i+1/2,j,k} \right) 
+ \frac{1}{\Delta y} \left( \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{y,i,j-1/2,k} - \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{y,i,j+1/2,k} \right) 
+ \frac{1}{\Delta z} \left( \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{z,i,j,k-1/2} - \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{z,i,j,k+1/2} \right) + \overline{\mathbf{S}}_{i,j,k},$$
(13)

where  $\overline{\bf U}$  is the cell averaged value of the conservative variables.  $\overline{\bf F}_x$ ,  $\overline{\bf F}_y$ , and  $\overline{\bf F}_z$  are the average convection flux over the cell faces  $x_{i\pm 1/2}$ ,  $y_{j\pm 1/2}$ , and  $z_{k\pm 1/2}$ , respectively. They are given by

$$\overline{\mathbf{U}} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_{1}\rho_{1} \\ \alpha_{2}\rho_{2} \\ \rho u \\ \rho v \\ \rho E \\ \alpha_{1} \end{pmatrix}; \quad \overline{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{U}) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \alpha_{1} \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right) \\ \alpha_{1} \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right) \\ \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{x}(\mathbf{U}) = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_{1}\rho_{1}u \\ \alpha_{2}\rho_{2}u \\ \rho u^{2} + p \\ \rho uw \\ \rho Eu + pu \\ \alpha_{1}u \end{pmatrix}; \quad \overline{\mathbf{F}}_{y}(\mathbf{U}) = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_{1}\rho_{1}v \\ \alpha_{2}\rho_{2}v \\ \rho vu \\ \rho vv \\ \rho Ev + pv \\ \alpha_{1}v \end{pmatrix}; \quad (14)$$

$$\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{z}(\mathbf{U}) = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_{1}\rho_{1}w \\ \alpha_{2}\rho_{2}w \\ \rho wu \\ \rho wu \\ \rho wv \\ \rho wu \\ \rho wv \\ \rho Ew + pw \\ \alpha_{1}w \end{pmatrix}.$$

The source term  $\overline{S}$  in the advection equation of volume fraction is discretized following the work of Johnsen and Colonius<sup>35</sup> as

$$(\alpha_{1}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})_{i,j,k} = \frac{(\alpha_{1})_{i,j,k}}{\Delta x} \left(\overline{u}_{i+1/2,j,k} - \overline{u}_{i-1/2,j,k}\right) + \frac{(\alpha_{1})_{i,j,k}}{\Delta y} \left(\overline{v}_{i,j+1/2,k} - \overline{v}_{i,j-1/2,k}\right) + \frac{(\alpha_{1})_{i,j,k}}{\Delta z} \left(\overline{w}_{i,j,k+1/2} - \overline{w}_{i,j,k-1/2}\right).$$
(15)

The spatial discretization method of the convection fluxes  $\overline{F}$  and velocity  $\overline{u}$  at the cell faces will be detailed in the following sections.

Finally, the strong stability-preserving (SSP) Runge–Kutta scheme  $^{54}$  is employed for the temporal integration to solve the ordinary differential equations

$$\mathbf{U}_{ij,k}^{(1)} = \mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{n} + \Delta t \mathbf{L} (\mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{n}), 
\mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{(2)} = \frac{3}{4} \mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{n} + \frac{1}{4} \mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{(1)} + \frac{1}{4} \Delta t \mathbf{L} (\mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{(1)}), 
\mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{n+1} = \frac{1}{3} \mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{n} + \frac{2}{3} \mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{(2)} + \frac{2}{3} \Delta t \mathbf{L} (\mathbf{U}_{i,j,k}^{(2)}),$$
(16)

where L is the operator evaluating the numerical approximation of right-hand side in Eq. (13), the superscripts (1) and (2) denote the two intermediate sub-steps within one time step, and the superscripts n and n+1 are the time step indices. The time step size  $\Delta t$  is adaptively calculated based on the propagation velocity of the characteristic waves and the usual Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) criterion.

#### A. Constructing numerical flux

Commonly, the convection fluxes  $\overline{F}$  are calculated in an upwind manner by using the Riemann solver, straightfully. While this method demonstrates excellent shock-capturing capabilities in compressible flows, it exhibits inherent limitations in the accuracy of interface advection due to the gradual smearing of interfaces over time, which obviously deteriorates the computational results and limits the simulation time.

To improve the resolution of the interface, extensive efforts have focused on improving the accuracy of convection fluxes from the Riemann solver, with notable recent works implementing high-order or THINC reconstruction in the interfacial region. In contrast, this work intends to employ the low-order reconstruction while sharpening the interfaces with additional numerical fluxes, which can maintain the physical consistency between the equations.

He and  ${\rm Tan}^{51}$  derived a general theoretical formulation of interface-sharpening techniques for the five-equation model. The theoretical formulation is conservative in mass, momentum, and total energy. Moreover, it is asymptotically compatible with the thermodynamic mixture laws of the mixture model upon which it is constructed and independent of various specific numerical algorithms. Building upon this foundation, we formulate the numerical fluxes  $\overline{F}$  at the cell face with two components as follows:

$$\overline{\mathbf{F}} = \widehat{\mathbf{F}} + \widehat{\mathbf{G}},$$
 (17)

with

$$\widehat{\mathbf{F}} = \begin{pmatrix} \widehat{F}_{\alpha_{1}\rho_{1}} \\ \widehat{F}_{\alpha_{2}\rho_{2}} \\ \widehat{F}_{\rho u} \\ \widehat{F}_{\rho v} \\ \widehat{F}_{\rho E} \\ \widehat{F}_{\alpha_{1}} \end{pmatrix}; \quad \widehat{\mathbf{G}} = \begin{pmatrix} \widehat{G}_{\alpha_{1}\rho_{1}} \\ \widehat{G}_{\alpha_{2}\rho_{2}} \\ \widehat{G}_{\rho u} \\ \widehat{G}_{\rho v} \\ \widehat{G}_{\rho w} \\ \widehat{G}_{\rho E} \\ \widehat{G}_{\alpha_{1}} \end{pmatrix}, \tag{18}$$

where  $\widehat{\mathbf{F}}$  denote the upwind Riemann fluxes, while  $\widehat{\mathbf{G}}$  represent the consistent interface sharpening fluxes. The detailed mathematical formulation and implementation strategies of  $\widehat{\mathbf{F}}$  and  $\widehat{\mathbf{G}}$  are demonstrated in Secs. III B and III C, respectively.

#### B. Upwind Riemann fluxes $\hat{\mathbf{F}}$

The Riemann flux  $\widehat{\mathbf{F}}$  is computed following the classical Godunov-type method. This approach first reconstructs the left and right states, denoted as  $\mathbf{U}^L$  and  $\mathbf{U}^R$ , on both sides of the cell face through a selected reconstruction scheme, then solves the Riemann problem to obtain  $\widehat{\mathbf{F}} \approx \widehat{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{U}^L, \mathbf{U}^R)$ .

For the reconstruction of the left and right states  $\mathbf{U}^L$  and  $\mathbf{U}^R$  at the cell face, the present work employs primitive variable reconstruction to prevent the emergence of spurious oscillations near material interfaces, as outlined by Johnsen and Colonius.<sup>35</sup> The classical reconstruction schemes such as total-variation-diminishing (TVD)<sup>55</sup> and MUSCL can all be used without exception in the current framework.

Specifically, it should be noted that both  $\alpha_k \rho_k$  and  $\alpha_k$  exhibit significant gradients at the sharp interface. Following the procedure

outlined in Zhang et al.,  $^{46}$  we assume that  $\rho_1$  and  $\rho_2$  are constant within the interface cells during the reconstruction, while the other variables are reconstructed without such restriction. For the reconstruction in the x-direction, the interfacial cells are identified through the volume fraction criterion (the direction indices j and k are omitted)

$$\begin{cases} \varepsilon < (\alpha_1)_i < 1 - \varepsilon, \\ \left[ (\alpha_1)_{i+1} - (\alpha_1)_i \right] \left[ (\alpha_1)_i - (\alpha_1)_{i-1} \right] > 0, \end{cases}$$

$$(19)$$

where  $\varepsilon$  is a small positive parameter. Our numerical experiments demonstrate that parameter values within the range  $10^{-3} \le \varepsilon \le 10^{-5}$  have negligible effect on interface detection. In this study, a nominal value of  $10^{-4}$  is adopted, aligning with established implementations in prior literature. <sup>43,56</sup> This criterion can be directly extended to the *y*-and *z*-direction through coordinate permutation.

Considering the computational constraints of solving the Riemann problem precisely, we utilize the HLLC approximate Riemann solver to calculate the flux  $\hat{\mathbf{F}}$ . For the sake of simplicity to elucidate the solution methodology, the HLLC Riemann solver implementation along the x-direction is detailed below, with multi-dimensional extension achieved through directional splitting.

After obtaining the left and right states  $\mathbf{U}^L$  and  $\mathbf{U}^R$ , the HLLC solver with an adaptation to solve volume fraction equation<sup>35</sup> for a three-dimensional problem reads

$$\widehat{\mathbf{F}} = \frac{1 + sgn(s^*)}{2} \left( \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U}^L) + s^-(\mathbf{U}^{*L} - \mathbf{U}^L) \right) + \frac{1 - sgn(s^*)}{2} \left( \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U}^R) + s^+(\mathbf{U}^{*R} - \mathbf{U}^R) \right),$$
(20)

where the intermediate state  $\mathbf{U}^{*K}$  (K = L or R) is specified as

$$\mathbf{U}^{*K} = \frac{s^{K} - u^{K}}{s^{K} - s^{*}} \begin{pmatrix} (\alpha_{1}\rho_{1})^{K} \\ (\alpha_{2}\rho_{2})^{K} \\ \rho^{K}s^{*} \\ \rho^{K}v^{K} \\ \rho^{K}w^{K} \end{pmatrix} . \tag{21}$$

$$E^{K} + (s^{*} - u^{K}) \left(\rho^{K}s^{*} + \frac{p^{K}}{s^{K} - u^{K}}\right)$$

According to Davis,  $^{57}$  the left and right wave speeds can be estimated as

$$s^{-} = \min(0, s^{L}), \quad s^{+} = \max(0, s^{R}),$$
 (22)

$$s^{L} = \min(u^{L} - c^{L}, u^{R} - c^{R}),$$
  

$$s^{R} = \max(u^{L} + c^{L}, u^{R} + c^{R}).$$
(23)

The speed of the intermediate wave  $s^*$  is given by

$$s^* = \frac{p^R - p^L + \rho^L u^L (s^L - u^L) - \rho^R u^R (s^R - u^R)}{\rho^L (s^L - u^L) - \rho^R (s^R - u^R)}.$$
 (24)

Finally, the velocity at the cell face is evaluated as the same velocity as in the computed numerical flux for consistency

$$\overline{u} = \frac{1 + sgn(s^*)}{2} \left( u^L + s^- \left( \frac{s^L - u^L}{s^L - s^*} - 1 \right) \right) 
+ \frac{1 - sgn(s^*)}{2} \left( u^R + s^+ \left( \frac{s^R - u^R}{s^R - s^*} - 1 \right) \right).$$
(25)

While the above HLLC solver was originally proposed for Euler equations based on Rankine-Hugoniot conditions, Johnsen and Colonius<sup>35</sup> subsequently adapted it to the volume fraction equation to preserve the consistency between the equations. We can find that the adaptation establishes the numerical flux for volume fraction equation through the Riemann flux of mass conservative equation as follows:

$$\widehat{F}_{\alpha_1} = \widehat{F}_{\alpha_1 \rho_1} / \left( \frac{1 + sgn(s^*)}{2} \rho_1^L + \frac{1 - sgn(s^*)}{2} \rho_1^R \right). \tag{26}$$

Thus, the inherent dissipation in numerical flux for the volume fraction equation can be attributed to the approximate nature of Riemann solutions to some extent, which originates from both state reconstruction and approximate Riemann solution procedures. These inherent limitations constitute the primary challenge for maintaining interface sharpness, and we will next describe how to keep interface sharpness through supplemental numerical flux components while rigorously maintaining equation system consistency.

## C. Consistent interface sharpening fluxes $\widehat{G}$

Based on the methodological framework established by He and  $\operatorname{Tan}_{5}^{51}$  we construct the interface sharpening fluxes  $\widehat{G}$  as follows:

$$\widehat{\mathbf{G}} = \begin{pmatrix} \widetilde{\rho}_{1}J_{1} \\ \widetilde{\rho}_{2}J_{2} \\ \sum_{k=1}^{2} \widetilde{\rho_{k}}uJ_{k} \\ \sum_{k=1}^{2} \widetilde{\rho_{k}}vJ_{k} \\ \sum_{k=1}^{2} \widetilde{\rho_{k}}\widetilde{w}J_{k} \\ \sum_{k=1}^{2} \widetilde{\rho_{k}}\widetilde{E}_{k}J_{k} \\ J_{1} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{27}$$

Considering a cell face i + 1/2 in the *x*-direction (the direction indices *j* and *k* are omitted in this section), the operator  $\tilde{q}$  is defined for any variable *q* as

$$\widetilde{q}_{i+1/2} = \begin{cases} q_i, & \text{if} \quad \overline{u}_{i+1/2} > 0, \\ q_{i+1}, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}$$
(28)

where  $\overline{u}$  is obtained from the HLLC Riemann solver (Eq. (25)).  $J_k$  denotes an artificial volume flux whose definition is independent of the Riemann flux introduced in the last section. We define  $J_k$  only exists in the interfacial region, which is identified by the condition Eq. (19), and it has the following form:

$$(J_k)_{i+1/2} = (\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_k)_{i+1/2} \Big( \overline{u}_{i+1/2} (\widehat{\alpha}_k)_{i+1/2} - (\widehat{F}_{\alpha_k})_{i+1/2} \Big). \tag{29}$$

Here,  $\mathcal{H}_k$  is a characteristic function and  $(\overline{u}\widehat{\alpha}_k - \widehat{F}_{\alpha_k})$  represents an anti-diffusive flux defined to correct the numerical diffusion resulting from the upwind Riemann flux  $\widehat{F}_{\alpha_k}$ . In the present work, instead of using a high-order scheme or the steepness-adjustable harmonic limiter<sup>52</sup> to calculate  $\widehat{\alpha}$  in Eq. (29), we construct  $\widehat{\alpha}$  with a first-order downwind scheme inspired by the FCT-VOF scheme<sup>53</sup>

$$(\widehat{\alpha}_k)_{i+1/2} = \begin{cases} (\alpha_k)_{i+1}, & \text{if} \quad \overline{u}_{i+1/2} > 0, \\ (\alpha_k)_i, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
(30)

It should be noted that the upwind flux component within  $J_k$  introduces sufficient numerical diffusion to counteract the instability of the anti-diffusive flux, which is consistent with the FCT-VOF scheme<sup>53</sup> originally developed for pure interface advection. Next, the characteristic function  $\mathcal{H}_k$  requires careful formulation to simultaneously maintain interfacial sharpening efficacy and numerical stability throughout the computation process.

## D. Determination of the characteristic function $\mathcal{H}_k$

To properly determine the characteristic function  $\mathcal{H}_k$ , let us examine the final formulation of volume fraction flux. Assuming  $\overline{u}_{i+1/2}$  is positive, substitution of the upwind Riemann flux Eq. (26) and interface sharpening flux Eq. (29) into Eq. (17) yields the numerical flux of volume fraction equation

$$\begin{split} (\overline{F}_{\alpha_{k}})_{i+1/2} &= (\widehat{F}_{\alpha_{k}})_{i+1/2} + (\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_{k})_{i+1/2} \Big( \overline{u}_{i+1/2} (\widehat{\alpha}_{k})_{i+1/2} - (\widehat{F}_{\alpha_{k}})_{i+1/2} \Big) \\ &= \overline{u}_{i+1/2} \Big[ (\alpha_{k}^{L})_{i+1/2} + (\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_{k})_{i+1/2} \Big( (\alpha_{k})_{i+1} - (\alpha_{k}^{L})_{i+1/2} \Big) \Big]. \end{split} \tag{31}$$

It can be seen that the resulting flux is essentially a TVD structure, where a portion of anti-diffusive component is incorporated into the low-order upwind scheme in such a way that second-order accuracy is preserved without creating unphysical oscillations. Thus,  $\mathcal{H}_k$  can be seen as a slope limiter dependent on the ratio  $r_i = \frac{(\alpha_k)_i - (\alpha_k)_{i-1}}{(\alpha_k)_{i+1} - (\alpha_k)_i}$ , and it should satisfy two essential criteria as follows.

![](_page_6_Figure_12.jpeg)

**FIG. 1.** Characteristic function  $\mathscr{H}_k(r)$  profiles in one-dimensional configuration. The black dotted line is the TVD upper constraint. The inset depicts the asymptotic behavior of  $\mathscr{H}_k(r)$  as the variable r approaches zero.

- Bounded variation:  $\mathcal{H}_k$  remains confined within the TVD region as  $0 < \mathcal{H}_k < 1$ .
- Asymptotic behavior: ℋ<sub>k</sub> approaches unity when r = 1 while diminishing to zero as r → 0 and r → ∞.

where the first criterion preserves numerical stability, and the second maximizes the interface sharpening effect in interfacial regions while ensuring a gradual reduction of this effect at distances from the interface. Based on these requirements, we construct  $\mathcal{H}_k$  formulation for the current methodology as follows:

$$(\mathcal{H}_k)_i = \left(1 - \left(\frac{1 - |r_i|}{1 + |r_i|}\right)^4\right) |(n_x)_i|.$$
 (32)

Figure 1 depicts the characteristic function [Eq. (32)] profiles in one-dimensional configuration. Specifically, we have also incorporated the interfacial geometric features through unit normal vectors in multi-dimensional implementations. The unit normal vector  $\mathbf{n} = (n_x, n_y, n_z)$  in each interfacial cell is calculated with the mixed-Youngs-centered (MYC) scheme proposed by Aulisa *et al.*. <sup>58</sup>

Next, we provide a stability analysis for the characteristic function in Eq. (32). Considering a transported scalar  $\alpha$  exhibiting a smooth and monotonic profile at time step n and advected with positive velocity  $\overline{u}$  as depicted in Fig. 2. The update of  $\alpha_i$  from time step n to n+1 is formulated as

$$\alpha_{i}^{n+1} = \alpha_{i}^{n} - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left( \overline{F}_{i+1/2}^{n} - \overline{F}_{i-1/2}^{n} \right)$$

$$= \alpha_{i}^{n} - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left( \widehat{F}_{i+1/2}^{n} - \widehat{F}_{i-1/2}^{n} \right) - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left( J_{i+1/2}^{n} - J_{i-1/2}^{n} \right), \quad (33)$$

where the interface sharpening flux at cell face, denoted  $J_{i+1/2}$ , can be seen as an anti-diffusive flux  $A_{i+1/2}$  constrained by a limiter  $C_{i+1/2}$ , with the following relationship:

$$J_{i+1/2} = C_{i+1/2} \left( \overline{u} \widehat{\alpha}_{i+1/2} - \widehat{F}_{i+1/2} \right) = C_{i+1/2} A_{i+1/2} \quad 0 \le C_{i+1/2} \le 1.$$
(34)

Given that  $\alpha$  is monotonically decreasing, the sign of anti-diffusive flux A is opposite to the upwind flux  $\widehat{F}$ . We define the net anti-diffusive fluxes into and out of cell i as  $A_{in} = \max(0, A_{i-1/2}) - \min(0, A_{i+1/2})$  and  $A_{out} = \max(0, A_{i+1/2}) - \min(0, A_{i-1/2})$ , respectively. Assuming the low-order upwind flux  $\widehat{F}$  would not result in any overshoot or undershoot, the constraint ensuring  $\alpha_i^{n+1}$  does not exceed the local maximum value  $\alpha^{\max_i} = \max(\alpha_{i-1}^n, \alpha_i^n, \alpha_{i+1}^n)$  nor fall below the local minimum value  $\alpha^{\min_i} = \min(\alpha_{i-1}^n, \alpha_i^n, \alpha_{i+1}^n)$  can be expressed as

![](_page_6_Figure_24.jpeg)

**FIG. 2.** One-dimensional profile of the transported scalar  $\alpha$  at time step n.

$$C_{i\pm 1/2} \le \min(R_{in}, R_{out}, 1),$$
 (35)

$$R_{in} = \frac{\alpha_i^{\text{max}} - \left(\alpha_i - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left(\widehat{F}_{i+1/2} - \widehat{F}_{i-1/2}\right)\right)}{\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} A_{in}},$$
 (36)

$$R_{out} = \frac{\left(\alpha_i - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \left(\widehat{F}_{i+1/2} - \widehat{F}_{i-1/2}\right)\right) - \alpha_i^{\min}}{\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} A_{out}},$$
 (37)

where  $R_{in}$  and  $R_{out}$  represent the fractional upper bounds that constrain C to prevent overshoot and undershoot at cell i, respectively. Considering the profile of  $\alpha$  shown in Fig. 2, the right-hand side of equations can be simplified as

$$R_{im} = \frac{\alpha_{i-1} - \left(\alpha_{i} - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \overline{u} \left(\alpha_{i+1/2}^{L} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}\right)\right)}{-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \overline{u} \left(\alpha_{i+1} - \alpha_{i+1/2}^{L}\right)}$$

$$= \frac{\alpha_{i} - \alpha_{i-1}}{\alpha_{i+1} - \alpha_{i+1/2}^{L}} \left(\frac{\Delta x}{\overline{u} \Delta t}\right) - \frac{\alpha_{i+1/2}^{L} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}}{\alpha_{i+1} - \alpha_{i+1/2}^{L}}$$

$$\geq \frac{\alpha_{i} - \alpha_{i-1}}{\alpha_{i+1} - \alpha_{i}} \left(\frac{\Delta x}{\overline{u} \Delta t}\right) - \frac{\alpha_{i+1/2}^{L} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}}{\alpha_{i+1} - \alpha_{i}}$$

$$\approx \left(\frac{\Delta x}{\overline{u} \Delta t} - 1\right) r_{i}, \qquad (38)$$

$$R_{out} = \frac{\left(\alpha_{i} - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \overline{u} \left(\alpha_{i}^{L} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}\right) - \alpha_{i+1}}{-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \overline{u} \left(\alpha_{i} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}\right)} - \alpha_{i+1}}{\frac{\Delta t}{\alpha_{i} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}} \left(\frac{\Delta x}{\overline{u} \Delta t}\right) + \frac{\alpha_{i+1/2}^{L} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}}{\alpha_{i} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}}}$$

$$\geq \frac{\alpha_{i+1} - \alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i} - \alpha_{i-1}} \left(\frac{\Delta x}{\overline{u} \Delta t}\right) + \frac{\alpha_{i+1/2}^{L} - \alpha_{i-1/2}^{L}}{\alpha_{i} - \alpha_{i-1}}$$

$$\approx \left(\frac{\Delta x}{\overline{u} \Delta t}\right) \frac{1}{r_{i}} + 1 > 1. \qquad (39)$$

On the one hand, the characteristic function Eq. (32) clearly satisfies  $H_k(r) < R_{out}$ . On the other hand, as shown in the inset of Fig. 1, this characteristic function satisfies  $H_k(r) < 8r$ . Consequently, the stability condition for Eq. (32) requires  $\Delta t < \Delta x/9\overline{u}$ . Within the scope of this work addressing compressible flows, where the time step is typically determined by the CFL number and global maximum wave speed  $\Delta t_{cfl} = \text{CFL} \cdot \Delta x/\text{max}(\overline{u} + c)$ , this stability condition is readily satisfied when CFL < 0.6 in the problems discussed in the next section.

As a result, the newly determined interface sharpening fluxes G in combination with the Riemann flux  $\widehat{F}$  provide a conservative and consistent interface-sharpening technique for the five-equation model. This methodology demonstrates significant improvements over the original MMACM framework, <sup>51</sup> featuring a simplified computational architecture while maintaining comparable computational efficiency. Numerical validation presented in the subsequent section confirms the scheme's capability to extremely sharpen the interface below two

grid cell widths while preserving robust performance across diverse flow regimes, therefore designating this enhanced methodology as MMACM-Ex to distinguish it from its predecessor.

#### **IV. NUMERICAL RESULTS**

In the present study, the numerical schemes are implemented on the open source software *Basilisk*, <sup>59–61</sup> which discretizes and solves the partial differential equations on Cartesian mesh. A key advantage of *Basilisk* is its implementation of a quad/octree-based adaptive mesh refinement method, which allows local grid refinement in regions close to interfaces and areas with large gradients in physical quantities. This significantly reduces computational resource requirements. Additionally, the MPI parallelism, combined with the tree-structured mesh, enhances the efficiency of the simulations.

To establish comparative benchmarks for the newly proposed MMACM-Ex, the results computed with the original MMACM<sup>51</sup> are also provided for comparison in the subsequent analyses. In addition, the TVD scheme with van Leer's limiter<sup>62</sup> is employed to reconstruct the left and right values on the cell face to exhibit the superiority of the current method in sharpening interface. The simulations are conducted with a fixed CFL number of 0.4.

## V. CONCLUSION

We have presented a novel high-fidelity numerical method for compressible multiphase flow governed by the five-equation model. The method employs a composite numerical flux formulation that integrates the upwind Riemann flux components with the interfacesharpening flux contributions. The upwind Riemann flux is computed using the HLLC Riemann solver, while the interface sharpening flux is constructed in a consistent formulation. This innovative approach can effectively and robustly counteract numerical dissipation during volume fraction advection through two critical mechanisms: an antidiffusive correction employing a controlled downwind scheme, and a systematically designed characteristic function ensuring sustained interface sharpness throughout simulations. The numerical framework is characterized by its conservative nature and can easily handle complex topological changes in fluid interfaces. In addition, the proposed

The proposed MMACM-Ex methodology has been rigorously validated through comprehensive one-, two-, and three-dimensional benchmark tests. These evaluations demonstrate its exceptional interface-resolution capabilities and seamless compatibility with complex equations of state. The results agree well with the experimental data and the numerical solutions from the literature, showcasing superior solution quality compared to the original MMACM. Future work will focus on incorporating viscosity, surface tension, and phase change effects, further enhancing its versatility and applicability in a wider range of scenarios.

The proposed interface sharpening methodology also demonstrates substantial potential for industrial applications, particularly in detonation flow simulations involving energetic materials and combustion processes within propulsion systems. In these scenarios, the fidelity of material interface evolution such as fuel droplet atomization and oxidizer mixing emerges as critical performance determinants, and interface smearing will induce erroneous mass fraction distributions that degrade chemical reaction kinetics modeling precision.

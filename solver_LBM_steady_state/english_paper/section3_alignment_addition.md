### Directional origin of the reduced speed-up for wall-bounded shear flows

The reduced speed-up observed for the channel and Couette flows in Table 4 is examined directly by comparing, at successive outer iterations, the correction direction δf proposed by the preconditioned Newton step (Section 2.4) with the true remaining error, defined as the difference between the current iterate and the converged fixed point. The cosine of the angle between these two vectors indicates whether the correction points, even approximately, toward the solution; a value near $+1$ indicates a reliable direction, a value near $0$ indicates no directional information, and a negative value indicates that the correction opposes the direction actually required.

Figure 10 reports this cosine for plane Poiseuille flow and the T-junction case, sampled every eight outer iterations. For the T-junction case the cosine remains positive throughout, averaging $0.66$ over the iterations sampled. For plane Poiseuille flow, by contrast, the cosine oscillates between $-0.90$ and $+0.89$ with an average near zero ($-0.05$), and its sign reverses over stretches of tens of iterations. The correction is therefore, on average, uninformative for this case: roughly half of the outer iterations propose a direction that opposes the true error, even though the admissibility gate of Section 2.4 prevents any such iteration from being accepted in place of a worse update.

This behavior is consistent with the spectral construction of Section 2.3, which linearizes about a single spatially uniform reference state under an assumed periodic boundary condition and therefore does not reproduce nonperiodic (wall) boundaries exactly. For a wall-bounded shear flow, the dominant component of the residual changes character as convergence proceeds — from the bulk velocity magnitude, to the curvature of the developing profile, to near-wall detail — so that a single fixed linearization is only intermittently aligned with the direction currently required. The T-junction geometry's dominant slow mode, governed by redistribution of mass and momentum across the junction, retains a more consistent spatial character throughout the iteration, for which the same fixed linearization remains a closer approximation. This directional instability, rather than a uniformly small correction magnitude, is the primary reason the channel and Couette flows require many more outer iterations to reach the convergence criterion than the mask-bounded flows of comparable grid size (Section 3.2), and hence the reason their speed-up over the native operator is markedly smaller in Table 4.

The shaded region marks negative alignment, where the correction opposes the direction required.

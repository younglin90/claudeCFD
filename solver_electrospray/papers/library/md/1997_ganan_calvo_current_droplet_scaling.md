1. INTRODUCTION

Among the distinctive properties of the atomization of a liquid by electrostatic means are: (1) monodispersity of the primary droplets; (2) high net charge on the surface of the generated droplets; and (3) controllable droplet size by varying either the electrosprayed flow rate or the electrical conductivity of the liquid to be electrosprayed. Because of these properties electrostatic atomization has been applied to several fields: production of ceramic powders (Rulison and Flagan, 1994; Slamovich and Lange, 1988); production of aerosol standards (Meesters et al., 1992); application of coatings (Seaver and Eckhardt, 1988); dispersion of pesticides (Morton, 1982); space thrusters (Bartoli et al., 1984); and mass spectrometry (Fenn et al., 1989). Usually, the electroatomization technique consists in the slow injection of a liquid through a capillary, electrified needle. For a certain range of both the applied voltage and the injected flow rate values, one finds a steady, special regime [the so-called cone-jet mode after Cloupeau and Prunet-Foch (1989)] m which the liquid is emitted from the vertex of a steady, almost conical, electrified meniscus in the form of an extremely thin, capillary, charged jet that breaks up into a fine spray of droplets with the above-mentioned distinctive characteristics. Since the first pioneering experimental studies by Zeleny (1914, 1915, 1917) earlier in this century until the work by Vonnegut and Neubauer (19.52) noticing the monodispersity of the droplets of an electrostatic spray, very little improvement in the knowledge of this subject was achieved. Thereafter, Taylor (1964) gave, for perfectly conducting liquids, a first explanation of the conical shapes as a hydrostatic balance between electrical and surface tension forces. Experimental investigations on the stability limits of the cone-jet mode as well as those on the dependence on the liquid properties, liquid flow rate and electrostatic conditions of the current and droplet size emitted from an electrified conical point have been done by Mutoh et al. (1979), Smith (1986), Hayati et al. (1987a, b), Cloupeau and Prunet-Foch (1989, 1990, 1994) among others. A great step forward in the field of electrospray has been recently made by Fernandez de la Mora and Loscertales (1994) who found the scaling law of the spray current emitted from

In between, there is a transition region of the jet at which both conduction and advection are of the same order. The importance of this region with characteristic axial and radial lengths LO and R, respectively lies in the fact that under some conditions (see Section 3), the total emitted current (an eigenvalue of the problem) is determined in it.

4. EXPERIMENTAL

The experimental set-up is essentially the same as those used by previous workers (Jones and Thong, 1971; Fernandez de la Mora and Loscertales, 1994; Gomez and Tang, 1994). Liquid contained in a small reservoir is forced by pressurized air through a narrow Teflon tube (id. N 0.32 mm ) whose length ranges from 20 to a few metres, Fig. 3a. The tube ends up at a charged capillary needle (id. z 0.7 mm, o.d. N 1 mm) connected to an electric potential of several kV relative to a perpendicular flat ground electrode located at a distance of a few centimetres from the end of the needle. The needle exit and the free surface of the liquid in the reservoir were kept at the same height throughout the experiments. Within the range of the experimental values, the flow rate Q varies linearly with the pressure difference across the feeding line AP, Q = a AP. The dimensional constant a is found from calibration of Q and AP. AP is measured with a mercury manometer, and the flow rate is found by measuring the time that an air bubble introduced on purpose into the feeding line takes to travel a given length. Once constant a is known the measured value of AP directly yields the value of Q. The mass flow rate injected through the needle is approximately the same as that emitted from the cone vertex since the evaporated mass flow rate from the conical meniscus is much smaller than the injected flow rate Q. In effect, a simple estimation of the evaporated mass flow rate, assuming a liquid sphere of radius equal to the needle radius, shows that the evaporation effect is small for the cases considered in this work. In the most unfavourable case, methanol or heptane electrosprayed at flow rates close to the minimum one, we found that the evaporated mass rate is about 200 times smaller than the ejected mass flow rate. Thus, the main uncertainty in the flow rate determination, which is at most of the order of 15%, comes from the variations in the cross section of the commercial Teflon tube (maximum error of the order of 10%). In all experiments the cone-jet was operated at atmospheric pressure and room temperature. Then, AP (AP = PO - P,, PO being the stagnation pressure in the reservoir ) and the needle voltage yielded by a BERTAN 250B-1 OR high voltage power supply are the control variables in this experiment.

6. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

A theoretical and experimental investigation on the spray current and droplet size emitted from electrified menisci of several liquids in cone-jet mode has been carried out in order to obtain the scaling laws of the electrostatic atomization useful for controlled manipulation of charged aerosols produced by electrospraying of a liquid as well as to gain an insight on the basic mechanisms of the electrosprays. The effects of the liquid flow rate, permittivity, electrical conductivity, density of liquid and surface tension of the liquid-gas interface on the size and charge density of the droplets emitted from a cone-jet meniscus have been considered.
